![How much term insurance do you need? Most people need cover of 10 to 15 times their yearly income](/blog/images/how-much-term-insurance-hero.webp)

Most people need term insurance cover of **10 to 15 times their annual income**. A more accurate figure comes from your own numbers: the money your family would need to live on for as many years as they depend on you, plus your loans and big goals, minus what you've already saved.

That's the short answer. The rest of this guide shows three ways to work out your number, puts one family's figures through all three, and explains why the methods land surprisingly close together. By the end you'll have a cover amount you can defend, and you can check it against your own numbers in the [Insurance Need tool](/verdict/insurance-need).

## What term insurance is for (and what it isn't)

Term insurance does one job. If you die during the policy term, it pays a fixed sum to the people you name. If you outlive the term, it pays nothing. That's why it's cheap: you're paying only for the risk of dying during the term, with no savings plan attached. Our lesson on [term insurance basics](/learn/term-insurance-basics) covers how it works in more detail.

So the question isn't "how much should I save through insurance?" It's "how much money would my family need if my income stopped tomorrow?"

You almost certainly need term cover if someone depends on your income: a spouse, children, or parents you support. You also need it if you hold a loan that your family would have to repay, like a home loan in joint names. If you're single, have no dependants and owe nothing, you may not need it yet. Buying while you're young still locks in a lower premium, though, and that's worth considering if you expect dependants within a few years.

## Method 1: the income multiple

The simplest rule says to buy cover of **10 to 15 times your annual income**. Earn ₹10 lakh a year, and that's ₹1 crore to ₹1.5 crore of cover.

Where you sit in that range depends mostly on age. A 28-year-old with 30 working years ahead has far more future income to protect than a 50-year-old with 10, so younger buyers belong at the top of the range and older buyers at the bottom.

**Strength:** it takes ten seconds and gives you a sensible order of magnitude.

**Weakness:** it can't see your life. Two people earning ₹12 lakh get the same answer, even if one has a ₹50 lakh home loan and two young children and the other is debt-free with grown-up kids. Treat the multiple as a first guess, not a final answer.

## Method 2: human life value (HLV)

Human life value asks a different question: what is your future income worth to your family today?

In words, the method works like this:

1. Take your yearly income and subtract what you spend on yourself and what you pay in tax. What's left is the money your family actually loses each year if you're gone.
2. Count the years until you'd retire.
3. Add up that yearly figure across all those years, allowing for pay rises.
4. Convert the total into today's money (this is called discounting), because a lump sum paid now can be invested and grow.

The result is the lump sum that, invested sensibly, would replace your contribution to the household until retirement.

You don't need to do the discounting by hand. If you assume your income grows a little more slowly than the payout could earn, HLV usually comes out at **15 to 20 times the money you contribute to your family each year**, for someone with around 25 to 30 working years left. It falls quickly as the years left get fewer.

HLV matters for another reason: insurers use a version of it when they decide the maximum cover they'll approve for you. That's why your income proof matters when you apply.

**Strength:** it's tied to your real income and working years.

**Weakness:** like the income multiple, it ignores your loans, goals and savings.

## Method 3: needs-based (the one we recommend)

The needs-based method starts from what your family would actually need, not from what you earn:

**Cover = yearly essential expenses × years they'd depend on you + outstanding loans + big future goals − savings and investments you already have**

Each piece is worth getting right:

- **Yearly essential expenses.** Rent or household running costs, groceries, school fees, utilities, insurance premiums and transport. Leave out your own personal spending, and leave out your loan EMIs, because the next line clears those loans.
- **Years of dependence.** Until your youngest child would be financially independent, or until you'd have retired, whichever is longer.
- **Outstanding loans.** Your home loan, car loan or any other debt your family would otherwise have to keep paying.
- **Big future goals.** A child's higher education is the usual one. Use today's cost; we'll come back to inflation.
- **Savings and investments.** Money your family could actually use: mutual funds, fixed deposits, PPF, shares.

One thing to leave out: **cover from your employer's group policy.** It usually ends the day you leave the job, and you can't control when that happens. Count it as a bonus, not as part of your plan.

## A worked example: one family, three methods

Meet Rahul. He's 32, lives in Pune and earns ₹12 lakh a year. His wife works part-time, and they have a two-year-old daughter. He has a home loan with ₹40 lakh still to repay, savings and investments of ₹15 lakh, and ₹12 lakh of group cover through his employer. The family's essential spending, leaving out the EMI, is about ₹6 lakh a year.

**Income multiple.** 10 to 15 × ₹12 lakh = **₹1.2 crore to ₹1.8 crore**.

**Human life value.** After his own spending and tax, about ₹9 lakh of Rahul's income goes to the family each year. He has 28 working years left. Assuming his income rises 5% a year and the payout would earn 8% a year, the value of that stream today is about **₹1.65 crore**.

**Needs-based.**

- Living costs: ₹6 lakh × 20 years (until his daughter is about 22) = ₹1.2 crore
- Plus the home loan: ₹40 lakh
- Plus his daughter's education, in today's money: ₹30 lakh
- Minus savings and investments: ₹15 lakh
- Employer cover: not counted

Total: **₹1.75 crore**.

![Needs-based example: ₹1.2 crore living costs plus ₹40 lakh home loan plus ₹30 lakh education minus ₹15 lakh savings equals ₹1.75 crore of cover](/blog/images/term-insurance-needs-based-example.webp)

| Method | How it's worked out | Cover |
|---|---|---|
| Income multiple | 10–15 × ₹12 lakh | ₹1.2–1.8 crore |
| Human life value | ₹9 lakh a year for 28 years, income rising 5%, discounted at 8% | ≈ ₹1.65 crore |
| Needs-based | ₹1.2 crore living costs + ₹40 lakh loan + ₹30 lakh education − ₹15 lakh savings | ₹1.75 crore |

![Three methods, one answer: income multiple ₹1.2 to 1.8 crore, human life value about ₹1.65 crore, needs-based ₹1.75 crore](/blog/images/term-insurance-three-methods.webp)

All three land between about ₹1.6 crore and ₹1.8 crore. That agreement is the reassurance: when three different methods point to the same band, you can be confident the number isn't a guess. For Rahul, **₹1.75 crore to ₹2 crore** of cover, running until he's 60, is a sound choice.

A note on honesty: the "₹6 lakh × 20 years" line ignores both inflation and investment returns. It works because the two roughly cancel out if the payout is invested sensibly. If the money earns about one percentage point more than inflation each year, the living-costs figure needed falls slightly, to about ₹1.1 crore. If it sits in a savings account, it will run out early. So the method gives the right ballpark, provided the family invests the payout carefully.

## How much term insurance should I buy? Adjusting for your situation

Your number moves with your circumstances. The main ones:

- **Single income or dual income.** If you're the only earner, the full calculation applies. If your partner earns well, you can reduce the living-costs line by what their income would cover, and they should usually have their own policy too.
- **A running home loan.** Add the outstanding balance, not the original loan amount. As you repay it, your need falls.
- **Your children's ages.** Younger children mean more years of dependence, and usually a bigger education goal still ahead.
- **Inflation.** A cover that looks large today buys less each year. ₹1 crore in 20 years, at 6% inflation, buys roughly what ₹31 lakh buys today. Use the [Inflation Calculator](/inflation-calculator) to see what your cover will be worth later, and lean towards the higher end of your range if you're young.
- **How long the policy should run.** Until your youngest child is independent, or until you plan to retire, whichever is later. Cover to age 60 or 65 is common. Paying for cover into your 80s adds cost for years when, ideally, nobody depends on your income.

## Is there a minimum or maximum cover?

**Minimum.** There's no rule that says you must buy a certain amount, but each insurer sets a minimum sum assured for each plan, and online term plans often start in the tens of lakhs. Check the plan's brochure before you compare quotes.

**Maximum.** India's insurance regulator, IRDAI, doesn't set a cap on term cover. Insurers set their own limits through underwriting: they check your income, age and the cover you already hold across all policies, and they approve total cover only up to a multiple of your income. That multiple is higher for younger buyers and falls as you get older, and it varies from insurer to insurer. If you need more cover than one insurer will give you, you can usually split it across two policies, but the second insurer will still look at your total.

## Term insurance vs endowment vs ULIP

This is where many people go wrong, because all three are sold as "life insurance".

| | Term insurance | Endowment plan | ULIP |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it's for | Protection only | Protection plus guaranteed or bonus-based savings | Protection plus market-linked investing |
| Pays if you survive the term | Nothing | Maturity amount | Fund value |
| Cover for the same yearly premium | Very high | Low | Low |
| Typical returns on the savings part | None | Modest, often 4–6% a year after charges | Market-linked, after several layers of charges |
| Flexibility | Simple; easy to add a second policy | Exiting early is costly | Five-year lock-in |

The key row is the third one. For the premium an endowment plan charges for modest cover, a term plan buys many times more cover. An endowment's savings part also tends to grow slowly. That's why the usual advice is to **buy term insurance for protection and invest the difference separately**, for example through a mutual fund SIP. You get good protection and good investing, instead of a mediocre version of both.

There are cases where someone prefers an endowment plan, usually for forced discipline or a guaranteed figure. If you're weighing a specific policy, the [Term vs Endowment verdict](/verdict/term-vs-endowment) compares it with term-plus-investing using your own numbers.

## When to review your cover

Your need isn't fixed. Check it again when:

- you get married,
- you have a child,
- you take out a home loan or another large loan,
- you get a big pay rise,
- you pay off a major loan.

The first four usually mean more cover; the last means less. Increasing cover generally means buying a second policy rather than changing the old one, which is fine: many people hold two or three term plans bought at different stages of life. Some plans let you raise cover at milestones like marriage or a child's birth without fresh medical checks, so ask about it when you buy.

One simple step protects the payout itself. If you're a married man, you can buy your policy under the Married Women's Property Act, which means the money goes only to your wife and children and can't be claimed by creditors.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much term insurance do I need?
A common starting point is 10 to 15 times your annual income. For a more accurate figure, add your family's yearly essential expenses for as many years as they'd depend on you, plus your outstanding loans and big goals such as education, then subtract savings and investments you already have. Use the larger of the two if they differ a lot.

### How much term insurance should I buy at 30?
At 30 you have many working years ahead, so your cover should usually sit at the high end, around 15 times your income or more if you have a home loan or young children. A needs-based calculation is more precise. Buying at 30 also locks in a lower premium than buying later.

### How do I calculate the term insurance amount I need?
Multiply your family's yearly essential expenses by the number of years they'd depend on you. Add outstanding loans and future goals like a child's education. Subtract your existing savings and investments. Don't count your employer's group cover, since it usually ends when you leave the job. Round the result up to a standard policy size.

### Is my employer's group insurance enough?
Usually not. Group cover is often only one to three times your salary, and it typically ends when you leave or lose the job, which may be exactly when your family is most exposed. Treat it as a bonus on top of your own term policy, not as a replacement for one.

### How many term insurance policies can I have?
There is no legal limit on the number of term policies you can hold. You must tell each new insurer about the cover you already have, and insurers will only approve a total that is reasonable for your income and age. If you die during the term, each policy pays out its own sum assured.

### Which is better, term insurance or an endowment plan?
For most people, term insurance plus separate investing does both jobs better. Term gives far more cover for the same premium, and money invested on your own, for example in mutual funds, has typically grown faster than the savings part of an endowment plan. An endowment can suit someone who values a guaranteed payout and forced saving above returns.

## The bottom line

The right amount of term insurance is the amount that would let your family keep their life on track without you: their living costs for the years they'd depend on you, your loans cleared and their big goals funded, minus what you've already built. For most young families, that lands somewhere between 10 and 20 times income. Work it out, buy it while you're young and healthy, and review it when life changes.
