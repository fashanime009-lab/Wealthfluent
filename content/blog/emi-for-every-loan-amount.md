The amount scales directly with how much you borrow — a 1,000,000 loan on the same terms costs ten times that, about 9,000 a month. ## How to scale this to your actual loan amount

The table is built around a clean unit — 100,000 — specifically so it scales by simple multiplication. Find your rate and tenure, read the EMI per 100,000, then multiply by how many 100,000s you're actually borrowing.

**Worked examples**, all read straight from the table above:

- **10 lakh (1,000,000)** at 9% for 20 years: 900 × 10 = **about 9,000/month**
- **20 lakh (2,000,000)** at 8.5% for 20 years: 868 × 20 = **about 17,360/month**
- **50 lakh (5,000,000)** at 8.5% for 20 years: 868 × 50 = **about 43,400/month**
- **1 crore (10,000,000)** at 8.5% for 20 years: 868 × 100 = **about 86,800/month**
- **500,000** at 9% for 20 years: 900 × 5 = **about 4,500/month**
- **300,000** at 7% for 30 years (a typical long-tenure mortgage): 665 × 3 = **about 2,000/month**

## How EMI is actually calculated

Every number above comes from the standard reducing-balance formula every bank and home loan uses:

**EMI = [P × R × (1 + R)^N] ÷ [(1 + R)^N − 1]**

Here, **P** is the loan amount, **R** is the monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100), and **N** is the number of monthly payments (years × 12). "Reducing balance" means interest is charged only on what you still owe, not on the original loan amount — which is why your EMI stays flat but the split between interest and principal inside it shifts every month: mostly interest at the start, mostly principal by the end.

A shorter tenure costs more per month and far less overall; a longer one is the opposite. There's no free lunch, only a trade-off between monthly comfort and total cost.

## Flat rate vs reducing balance — the trap to watch for

Some lenders, especially for personal, vehicle, or gold loans, quote a "flat rate" instead of the reducing-balance rate used throughout this article. A flat rate charges interest on the full original loan amount for the entire tenure, even though you're steadily paying it down — which makes the real, reducing-balance-equivalent rate significantly higher than the flat number quoted. Always ask a lender to state their rate on a reducing-balance basis before comparing it against the table above; comparing a flat-rate offer directly to these figures will understate what you're actually paying.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the EMI for a 10 lakh loan at 9% for 20 years?
About 9,000 a month (900 per 100,000 borrowed, × 10). Use the table above for other rates and tenures, or the [EMI Calculator](/emi-calculator) for an exact figure.

### What is the EMI for a 50 lakh home loan?
At 8.5% over 20 years, about 43,400 a month. At 9% it's about 45,000; at 8% it's about 41,800. Home loan rates and the exact amount both move the figure, so check the full table above for your specific terms.

### Does a longer tenure reduce my EMI?
Yes, but not proportionally, and it increases total interest significantly. Going from a 10-year to a 20-year tenure at 9% lowers the monthly EMI by about 29% but more than doubles the total interest paid over the life of the loan.

### Is a flat interest rate the same as a reducing-balance rate?
No. A flat rate is charged on the original loan amount for the whole tenure; a reducing-balance rate (what this table and nearly all home loans use) is charged only on the outstanding balance. A flat rate quoted at the same number as a reducing rate is always more expensive — ask your lender which basis they're quoting.

### How much EMI can I afford?
A common guideline is to keep all your EMIs combined at 30–40% of your monthly income or less. The [Home Affordability Calculator](/home-affordability-calculator) checks a specific home price against your income using this rule.

## The bottom line

EMI scales predictably with loan amount, rises with rate, and falls — slowly, not proportionally — as tenure stretches out.
